1. Purpose of this document

This document explains what EZvoxa is, where it came from, who it is designed for and why. We will touch on many topics, including what it does, what it deliberately does not try to do, and how it is meant to be positioned alongside, not in place of, traditional AAC systems and other speech-language services. I am not trying to state a case for its use, I am just telling the story of how it came to be, the results I have witnessed first-hand, and the tireless amount of research that went into literally every step of its build, with research citations included so every claim can be checked and verified.

One thing about myself, the author, so you can weigh what follows in its proper context. I am not a speech-language pathologist. Nor am I a BCBA or an educator. I am simply a father, who has spent the past 16 years in silence with my son. Learning what every look, every nuance of his facial expressions, his eyes and his body language are trying to tell me. I have lived a lifetime tortured by how I can help unlock all that is trapped inside him. His knowledge, his abilities, and most of all – his love. I have always felt that my son is locked inside himself. Finding the way to help him out is my life’s mission. It is the reason I get up in the morning.

After 16 years of being the only interpreter in every room he enters, of watching professionals with PhDs and Master’s degrees in neuroscience and education try every tool they have come up with, I accepted what I always knew. The best experts for my son live within the same walls he does. There are things about a nonverbal teen that may or may not show up in a 45-minute clinic evaluation, or a 30-minute speech therapy session (twice a week if you are lucky), or during his 60-minute twice-yearly exam by his pediatrician. Even the 6 hours or so a day spent in school, which is broken into 6 or 7 periods with different teachers and aides.

And then there are the things that only show up when he wakes up in the morning, while he is playing at home on the weekends or during the summer. When he is rummaging through my kitchen at dinner time, or 2 a.m. while I’m sleeping. I am not limited by time. We have all the time in the world and that is the vantage point from which this document was written. Where the published evidence speaks, I cite it; where I am describing what I watched happen to my own son, I say so.

And I would put those observations up against anything that any study or any expert has ever cited. I am smart enough to know one thing. If it helps Evan, it will help others, because that is who Evan is. It is who he has always been. And anyone who has spent any time with Evan at all knows this to be true.

2. What is EZvoxa?

My son, Evan Zachary, was born in August of 2010. His story started as that of any child. Through 12 to 16 months, he hit every milestone, on time or early. And then something changed.

As the father of two older sons, I knew it the minute I saw it.

Evan began to regress. After starting to say Mama and Dada, and happily playing with his toys, I noticed a regression in his attempts to speak. He still played with his toys but not the way he did before. He was beginning to walk, having never really crawled, and when he did walk, it was always on his tippy toes.

It was at his 18-month check-up that I first brought it up to his pediatrician. While it was too early to diagnose, the signs were starting to come into focus and by his 2-year-old check-up, the pediatrician began to see the same signs, but it was still too soon to know for sure. That would change during the following year, when we received an official diagnosis. Evan was Autistic and furthermore, nonverbal. He had fine motor skill delays, sensory issues and was developmentally delayed.

The Autism diagnosis was crushing, but the thought he would never speak? Well, that was a gut punch that literally drains all the air from your lungs, and you feel in that moment as though you may never breathe again.

Like all parents, we scrambled to learn anything, everything we could about Autism Spectrum Disorder. We entered him in a pre-school program built to help nonverbal children. Back then, as it is now, we were told that early intervention was his best hope.

We started home therapy. And sign language was suggested. Watching how quickly he would catch on to new signs was a clear indication, to me anyway, that he was determined to find a way to express what was inside him. He caught on so quickly that the therapist told us that what would take most of a month to get her other clients to learn one sign, Evan was picking up one or two each week. While that would make most parents proud, we were hesitant. To his mother, going all out teaching Evan how to sign meant we were giving up too soon on him ever speaking. Anyone in this room who has sat across from the parents of a nonverbal child has heard that fear. Evan was her first born. Her entire world revolved around him. And I respected how it made her feel. So, we continued with basic signing but continued to put a lot of energy into trying to get him to make basic sounds. I mention all this because we experienced the field’s most common misconception before we heard the research answer to it. The evidence says alternative communication supports speech development rather than hindering it (AssistiveWare, n.d.; ASHA Perspectives).

We were not giving up on his voice. We were building the road to it.

We did not know for certain what we were dealing with until Evan was about 7. That is when the diagnosis of Speech Apraxia came into focus.

I was able to contact a speech pathologist from Seattle, who had developed a learning tool called Gemiini. Evan had tried it when he was younger and it helped him, for a while. She is a mother of 7 children and 4 were on the spectrum – some nonverbal. As we met by video so she could observe Evan, it took her less than 3 minutes to start telling me everything about him - everything. It was as if she knew him when in fact, she was quite familiar with childhood apraxia of speech. His mind knows what he wants to say; but the signal never gets from that part of his brain to his vocal cords to produce the words. This is what ultimately led to a speech therapist introducing him to a dedicated AAC device, an iPad running LAMP Words for Life, serious and well-regarded software, billed at over $4,000 and thankfully covered by his Medicaid.

I want to be unambiguous about that device: it worked. It taught a small boy that words exist, that they can be chosen, that they can be combined to mean something. It gave him language, and his mother and I were and are forever grateful for it and for every therapist and teacher who worked with him to utilize it.

Through middle school and into his freshman year of high school, his mother and I watched his progress not just stall but come to a near standstill. Evan had learned to communicate with his eyes and expressions. He had learned to understand others through emotional attunement – the art of reading a person’s energy. And, in fact, I believe he had also learned how to communicate by returning this energy, specifically with his peers at school.

While he was never one to truly initiate with his AAC device unprompted, now he was ignoring the device altogether. He had become reliant on his ability to sense what a person was saying, either through their energy, their facial expressions or even the tone of their voices. Anyone who has spent 10 minutes around Evan, his family, his teachers, his friends, even strangers in a checkout line… they all walk away saying the same thing: there is so much more in there. His pediatrician often tells me he cannot wait until the day we are able to unlock his cognitive ability.

Agreeing that his progress had stalled, I asked his mother to let me take Evan full time and homeschool him for 10th grade. She was hesitant but relented. And for the next year I tried to learn everything I could about my son. I had doctors run labs we had never run before. I took him to a geneticist in Miami. At home, I put all my focus on communication. Why was it that now, even when prompted, Evan was back to walking into a room and pointing? This was no longer him stalling out. This was regression.

Given the choice, Evan ignored the device and went back to grunting and pointing at things, whether it be food he wanted me to cook in the kitchen or pointing at his bathing suit to let me know he wanted to go to the pool.

And during the times when I actually made him use his AAC device, I cared less and less about watching him build a sentence to tell me something. Instead, I watched the frustration build in his face as he navigated in and out of a dozen screens to construct something as simple as “I’m tired, can I go to bed?”

A sentence any other teenager says in two seconds cost him a minute of menu diving, every night, for a thought he’d expressed on that device a thousand times. It hit me one night like a ton of bricks to the face. I had seen this before. When Evan was in elementary school they started him in an ASD self-contained classroom. I would see children act out, mostly due to an inability to control their emotions. That was never Evan. He wakes up happy every day of his life. And when he is overstimulated, he will walk away for a moment before returning. But there were other children whose outbursts were born of frustration.

Angry that they were not being heard. Angry that they were being misunderstood. Angry that they were being asked to do the same task over and over again. And that’s what this was. I had seen my son build sentences daily since he was in 4th or 5th grade. I would watch as he meticulously found a word, clicked it, then watch as the board took him back to the home page to go looking for another until he painstakingly had enough words to say what he wanted to say. Now, this was fine when he was younger and the AAC device was more than just a way to communicate. It was also a way to instruct how to properly construct a sentence. But I asked myself, when I was a teenager, how would this make me feel?

The answer was clear. He wasn’t failing the device, the device was pricing him out of his own voice, and he responded the way anyone would: he stopped paying.

Bringing him home for a season of learning, with no IEP and no speech or OT sessions, had led to an important discovery. And one that just might change everything.

I respect the professionals who introduced Evan to his AAC device. The work they do, the patience they show not just with the children they work with but with us parents, is Godlike. But a decade of using the standard playbook had brought him to a plateau.

I decided it was time for me to be the student and allow Evan to be my teacher.

I went into the lab and left no stone unturned. And that is where this app was built. And when the new school year began, Evan walked back into this district’s schools with it, but this time it was in his pocket.

The app came from a single question I forced myself to answer honestly. What matters more? Watching my son show me he can build a sentence word for word? Or giving him the freedom to express himself quickly, easily, so he could feel heard again.

What we never had was the easy, constant flow of his wants, his needs, his feelings… Of hearing his voice unprompted, when HE had something to say or express.

So we built something new, that he could hold in the palm of his hand and store in the front pocket of his shorts. Using sentences and phrases he actually uses, all at the touch of his fingers, with a color-coded sentence builder and a spelling keyboard still underneath for all the things nobody could script in advance AND to keep the AAC portion alive and functional. His buttons carry avatars of his family, his school, the actual food he eats. When it was time to choose how he would sound, I subscribed to an AI voice site and played dozens of voices to nearly no reaction until finally he smiled, giggled almost, and pointed. I played two more to no response then went back to the one he reacted to and got the same exact pointing and smile. For the first time, the voice coming out of his device was one he chose.

And then came the day I knew for certain we had something that would be life-changing for Evan and maybe others. I was early in the development of the app but I had shown it to Evan two or three times maybe.

One night after dinner I was working at my desk and Evan approached, pointed to my phone, opened the app himself, and scrolled to the button for TV shows, clicked Netflix and scrolled through all the shows and documentary posters I had loaded on the device until he found the show he wanted to watch and he pressed it. Unprompted. Not even sitting in the same room together. Nobody put the device in front of him; nobody cued him; he had something he wanted, and he came and got the tool to say it.

I waited 16 years for that moment. And that is the whole thesis of EZvoxa in a single moment: when the effort to say something is cheap enough, that he started saying it on his own.

EZvoxa (vox is Latin for voice) exists because others who would see us using it, at the pool, at the store, at his school, kept asking where they could buy it. The product came second. Evan came first, and he is still the first user of every update we make to the app.

3. Three weeks back at school, what his teachers are seeing

The strongest evidence I can offer does not come from within my house. It comes from inside other buildings, back in school around his peers and the teachers who missed him so much during his year away, and just three weeks into the school year:

“We’ve never seen him this engaged academically.” His teachers’ words, three weeks in, talking about the same student who had stalled somewhere between 7th and 9th grade.

He volunteered to demonstrate his talker to his classmates. A nonverbal student volunteered to stand in front of his classmates, using an overhead projector to present and show how his app works. Consider what that sentence would have sounded like a year ago.

He asks for help appropriately using his phone. A raised hand and “Can you help me?” where there used to be frustration or shutdown. His teachers did not have to learn a device to hear him.

He is doing academic work on it. The calculator and number boards carried his math work; the word tools carried his simile-and-metaphor homework. A student who cannot speak is now completing figurative-language assignments on his talker with very little assistance from others.

Outside school, strangers stop us to ask where they can buy it. In restaurants, stores, and waiting rooms. Every family that asks is asking for someone.

Three weeks is an anecdote, not a study, and I present it as exactly that. But it is an anecdote unfolding in this district’s own classrooms, observable by this district’s own staff, which is precisely why I believe a supervised conversation about what comes next is worth having.

4. What EZvoxa is

A phrase-first communication app for a phone or iPad: the user’s real, recurring sentences, organized into boards for family, food, school, feelings, health, places, and routines. Each easily spoken with a single tap.

A generative layer underneath: a color-coded sentence builder and an alphabet speller, so novel messages remain fully possible. Phrase-first does not mean phrase-only.

Personalized with real photographs. A generic symbol of a woman is not a student’s mother; a photograph of his mother is. Families put real faces and real places on the buttons, because for a non-reading user the picture is the button.

Fully functional offline. Everything the user has said before still speaks with no signal and no Wi-Fi, verified by testing with all network access disabled. School buildings with dead zones and restricted networks do not silence it.

Fully customizable. The device comes pre-loaded, but it is also fully customizable. Every family, therapist or school can make it their own. Add your own buttons with your own words and phrases. Put a real photograph on any of them: their grandmother's face, their actual bedroom, the exact snack they ask for. Or use approved avatars drawn of your child and their family, so the whole app is filled with pictures of them, not clip art of somebody else. Rearrange the home screen. Add people by name and photo. As their life changes, the app changes with them: new teachers, new foods, new interests, added in minutes by the people who know them best. No two EZvoxa apps look alike, because no two kids are alike.

Lockable for a dedicated device: a start screen and close flow designed for Guided Access, so a phone in a pocket cannot press its own buttons; parent/editor controls sit behind a password. The only thing that lives and functions on that iPhone is this app. Evan cannot use any other part of the iPhone.

An emergency button that alerts the user’s own family with a message and live location. It states in the app, and I will state here: it is not a substitute for calling 911.

Family features (premium upgrades): pre-built messages Evan can choose to either “speak” to a family member, or, if their phone number is loaded in the app, he can send those same messages to them by text message, voice messages from family members that play in their actual voices without Evan having to do anything but clear them from his screen once he hears them, synchronized editing across family phones, and age-appropriate synthetic voices rather than a generic robot.

5. What EZvoxa is not

I consider this section as important as any other in the document.

It is not a speech-generating device in the clinical or funding sense. It is not billed to insurance, not prescribed, and not positioned as durable medical equipment.

It is not a language-acquisition system. It does not teach core vocabulary, morphology, or syntax the way the established AAC platforms do, and it does not claim to. Those systems do that work well; EZvoxa presumes that work is substantially done.

It is not a replacement for a prescribed AAC system, and we will never tell a family to abandon one. Best practice in the field is multimodal: speech, sign, the dedicated device, and other tools together, with the user choosing the channel that fits the moment (Donaldson et al., 2021). EZvoxa is one more channel, not a substitute for the others.

It is not a replacement for speech-language services. Nothing about a faster way to say a sentence reduces a student’s need for the professionals who develop the language behind it.

It makes no clinical-outcome claims. Evan’s daily use is proof of existence, not a study. Formal review by speech-language pathologists is on our roadmap before we ever market this as anything more than what it is: a tool one family built and other families asked for.

6. Why ages 13-and-up?

The age line is not a marketing choice; it is the design thesis, and it rests on three legs.

First: acquisition and use are different jobs. Traditional AAC platforms are, rightly, teaching instruments. They exist to build language. EZvoxa is a using instrument that exists to lower the cost of language a person already has. A tool that optimizes for one-tap speed would be the wrong tool for a 6-year-old who still needs to assemble sentences because assembling them is how the learning happens. We say so plainly, to parents, on our own website: if your child is still learning to combine words, the prescribed device and the therapist remain the right answer.

Second: the rate problem grows up with the user. Adult and adolescent AAC users typically produce 12–18 words per minute against 125–185 for spoken conversation (Trnka et al., 2008), roughly a tenfold gap. A young child’s conversational partners wait; a teenager’s do not. By adolescence, the social cost of slow output compounds. Turns get skipped, conversations move on, and the user learns to say less. Pre-stored, self-chosen sentences attack precisely this, because with ease of use the user has more to say and isn’t that what we all want for our nonverbal teens and young adults?

Third: adolescence is where devices get abandoned. The foundational research on assistive-technology abandonment found roughly a third of devices completely abandoned and identified the leading predictors: the user’s opinion was not considered in selection, and the user’s needs changed (Phillips & Zhao, 1993). That is a description of an AAC teenager: the device was chosen for the child they were, their needs changed, and their own preferences, including the wish to carry what every other teenager carries, went unweighted. The field’s own literature credits the move to mainstream mobile devices with reducing the stigma and attitudinal barriers around AAC (McNaughton & Light, 2013). A phone in a pocket is not merely more convenient than a tablet in a bag; for a teenager, it is the difference between a device that marks him as being different and one that makes him look like everyone else in the hallway.

In one sentence: EZvoxa is for the person who already knows exactly what they want to say, and is tired of paying a ten-tap toll to say it, and that person is almost always thirteen or older. The same logic extends to adults who lose speech to stroke, ALS, or injury after a lifetime of language; an adult-specific edition for that population is in design now.

7. Building on traditional AAC

I want to address the concern I would have in your seat: that a parent-built app encourages families to walk away from clinically appropriate systems. Our position, stated publicly and built into the product:

Keep the device. The prescribed system remains the right tool for language growth, for therapy sessions, and for the messages EZvoxa’s phrase-first model does not cover. We tell families this explicitly.

Multimodal is the standard we build to. The field’s consensus is that users should routinely have multiple communication options and choose among them (Donaldson et al., 2021; AssistiveWare, n.d.). Adding a fast everyday channel is an application of that standard, not a departure from it.

The relationship is sequential, not rivalrous. The dedicated device taught Evan that sentences can be built. EZvoxa exists because he learned that lesson. One is the classroom; the other is the hallway, the bus, the McDonald’s counter, the 9 p.m. “I need help.” A district does not choose between them any more than it chooses between a reading curriculum and a library card.

Educators shaped the app already. The School board inside it has boards for teachers, office staff, Speech/OT, and the bus driver. Each opens phrases fitted to that relationship (“Can you say that again?”, “I finished my work”, “Can I go see the nurse?”). Plus spelling, numbers, a calculator, and colors. Teachers can be added by name and photograph, because “Mrs. Delgado” with her face on the button is findable by a student who does not read. Those photos can be uploaded only to the user’s device and removed anytime you want to take them off.

We encourage professional scrutiny and feedback. Formal SLP review is a committed step on our roadmap, and the reason I am in this meeting is that I would rather have educators inside this process than outside it. Criticism from this room makes the tool better for the students in it.

8. Security: how the app is built, and what that means at school

I am not a security company, so instead of assurances, here is exactly how the app is put together and what that does and does not protect. Everything below can be verified by your own staff.

It is one file with no third-party code running inside it. The entire app is a single web page. There are no advertising libraries, no analytics packages, no tracking scripts, and no outside code loaded while it runs. Your technology staff can confirm this in about a minute: open the app with the browser’s network inspector on, and watch it make no outside requests at all. I tested the app with all network access switched off and it launched and ran completely, which is only possible because nothing outside is required to run it.

There is nothing stored about a student to steal. No accounts, no usernames, no passwords for students, no email addresses, no grades, no attendance, no assessment data, and no identifiers of any kind. The app cannot tell you who is using it. Boards, phrases, and photographs live in the device’s own local storage and nowhere else. A person who took the device would see the same buttons the student sees, and nothing more.

Photographs never leave the device. A photo taken for a button is written to that device only. It is never uploaded, never included in anything shared between devices, and it is gone when the person is removed or the app is deleted.

The device is what is locked, and the school already knows how to do that. On a dedicated device the app is locked with iOS Guided Access, so the student cannot leave the app, browse the internet, or reach anything else on the device. The app adds its own start screen and close step so a device in a pocket or a backpack cannot press its own buttons. Editing is behind a password, so a student cannot change or delete their own board.

What travels, and only when the feature is used. Speaking a phrase in the AI voice sends the text of that phrase to our server so it can be spoken, over an encrypted connection, and it is cached so a repeated phrase is not sent again. An emergency alert sends the message and the location at that moment to the family contacts the parent set up. Sending a text message sends that message to the family number the parent entered. Nothing else is transmitted, and none of it is attached to a name or an identifier.

Where the secrets live. No phone numbers, keys, or account credentials exist anywhere inside the app. They are held as server settings the app cannot read, and the app sends only an internal reference, never a number. Everything is served over HTTPS.

How updates reach the device. The app updates itself from our hosting the next time it is opened. There is no separate installer, nothing for your staff to push, and no way for code from anywhere else to load into it.

My recommendation for a pilot, and why. Run the pilot devices with family sync turned off and the emergency alert not configured. In that state every device is entirely self-contained: it stores nothing off the device, sends nothing anywhere, and needs no network at all. It is the simplest configuration to approve and the easiest to verify. Sync and alerts are family features that belong on a parent’s own phone, not a classroom tablet.

What we do not have, stated plainly. We are a new company and we do not hold SOC 2 Type II or ISO 27001 certification. Many districts require those of vendors that store student personally identifiable information. My argument is that this app stores none, on any server, at any time. I will put that in writing, sign your district’s data privacy agreement, and answer any security questionnaire your staff sends. If your agreement requires that no student data is collected or retained, we can sign it, because it is simply true.

9. Privacy, safety, and practical questions

The questions I expect anyone interested in this app to ask, answered directly:

Accounts and data: there are none to speak of. No user accounts, no advertising, no analytics trackers, no sale of data, the app cannot identify its user. Board content and photographs stay on the device; they are not uploaded. The full privacy policy is public at ezvoxa.com/privacy.html.

Student photographs placed on buttons remain on that phone. Family Sync, when enabled by the family, synchronizes button text and settings, deliberately not photographs.

The emergency button alerts the family contacts the parent configured. It does not call 911, does not contact the school, and says so on its face. Location is used only at the moment of an SOS, never in the background.

Connectivity: core communication works entirely offline; school network restrictions do not affect it.

Hardware: it runs on the phone or iPad a family already owns. There is no hardware to procure, insure, or repair.

Cost: while we are still prepping to put this on the app store by October 2026, the final price will be a fraction of the cost of the full AAC device Evan had previously, which Medicaid was billed for at more than $4,000. We will also include an optional $12.99/month subscription that adds the server-backed family features (AI voices, in-app texting, voice messages, family sync). The emergency button works whether or not a family subscribes, and it always will. A hardship program for low-income families is part of the plan because the first user’s family knows what the four-thousand-dollar quote feels like. And if a school district would like to pilot the app, we would be more than willing to discuss a 90-day trial where we would add, move or take things out to make it meet your needs.

Platform: installable today from the web on iPhone and iPad; an App Store release is in preparation, with Android to follow.

10. What I am asking, and what I am not

I am not asking the district to purchase anything, endorse anything, or change any student’s IEP. I am asking for three things. First, meet your students where they are instead of making them use only “approved tools.” It is called a spectrum for a reason. Second, the chance to demonstrate the app as Evan actually uses it, and the professional reactions of the people in this room, especially where you think I am wrong. Third, if there is interest, a conversation about what a small, properly supervised pilot with appropriate students would need to look like for you to trust it. The teacher who asked me about her father is the reason I believe this conversation is worth an hour of your time.

One more thing

When Evan was 3, 4, 5, 6 years old, I put him to bed every night the same way. I would kiss him, tell him I loved him, and say the same words: I don’t know how, but someday, you and I are gonna change the world. Every single night. I had no idea how, or when, or in what way. I just always felt that something great would come from all of it. I believe this is the start of us keeping that promise.
