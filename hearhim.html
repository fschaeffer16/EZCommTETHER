Sixteen years
of silence.

My son Evan was born in August 2010. He has apraxia of speech: his mind knows exactly what he wants to say, and his mouth cannot make the words. This is our story. Read it through, then use the tabs above: you can touch his actual talker, and it will talk back.

Where it starts

It starts in preschool, with sign language.

He caught on quickly, quicker than anyone expected. And his mother was afraid of exactly that. She feared that signing meant we were giving up on him ever speaking, too soon. If you've sat across from parents of a nonverbal child, you have heard that fear before. We lived it before we ever heard the research answer to it.

We didn't know for sure what we were dealing with until he was seven: apraxia of speech. The words were in there. The path out wasn't.

The device that worked

A couple of years later, a speech therapist introduced him to a real AAC device. I'm grateful for it. It gave him language.

An iPad running LAMP Words for Life, serious and respected software, billed at over four thousand dollars. It taught a small boy that words exist, that they can be chosen, that they can be put together to mean something. Every therapist and teacher who put it in front of him has my thanks.

But it was built for the boy he was. And he grew up.

The stall

Through seventh, eighth, and ninth grade, I watched him stall. Not regress. Stall. The same boards, the same assembly, the same plateau. While everyone who has ever spent ten minutes around Evan, from teachers to family to strangers in a checkout line, walks away saying the same thing: there is somebody in there.

What I watched at home

Given the choice, Evan ignored the device and went back to grunting and pointing, the currency he and I had traded his whole life. It was faster. And when I made him use it, I watched the frustration build in his face as he navigated in and out of a dozen screens to construct something as simple as:

"I'm tired, can I go to bed."

A sentence any other teenager says in two seconds cost him a minute of menu-diving. Every night. For a thought he'd had a thousand times. He wasn't failing the device. The device was pricing him out of his own voice. And he responded the way anyone would: he stopped paying.

The decision

So I made the hardest call I've made as his father. I brought him home for a season. No IEP, no speech or OT sessions. Not because I don't value those professionals, but because a decade of the standard playbook had reached a plateau, and it was time for me to be the student and Evan to be my teacher. I ordered the lab work we had never done: genetic testing, to chase the why behind the apraxia instead of only managing the what. No stone unturned.

The question I finally forced myself to answer

What matters more: watching him build sentences, or hearing him?

The season at home

I didn't design this app. Evan guided me to it.

The homeschool season had one rule: I was the student. Evan doesn't explain himself in words. He explains himself in behavior. Which buttons he reached for. Which screens he abandoned. Where his finger hovered and where it flew. When something in the app wasted his time, he told me the only way he can: he stopped using it. Every one of those silences was a design review, and I treated it like one.

Not a gimmick. A method.

This isn't something a father built for his son. It's hundreds of design decisions, each one traced to a way to help unlock the cognitive abilities of the nonverbal.

The quick-word row exists because I paid attention to what he actually needed to say across his days: help, good, bad, restroom, thank you. Then I put those words one thumb-swipe from anywhere. The pictures are as large as the screen physically allows because Evan doesn't read: the picture is the button, and I measured his boards at his screen size, hunted down every layout where a border or a box was stealing pixels from the artwork, and rebuilt it. When a board was wrong, he showed me by ignoring it. When it was right, he showed me by using it without being asked.

I did the reading, too. The communication-rate literature. The device-abandonment research and its predictors. The field's own findings on why teenagers walk away from AAC. Every citation is in the written case statement that accompanies this page, and I'll happily be graded on it. But the peer reviewer who mattered most was sixteen, nonverbal, and brutally honest: he voted with his hands, every single day. This app is the tally.

His voice, his choice

When it came time to choose how he would sound, I didn't pick. I played him voices one after another and watched his face. He reached out and pointed at one. It wasn't close. For the first time, the voice coming out of his device was one he chose. A teenage boy's voice, not a grown man's, not a robot's.

The day I knew

He came to me, asked for my phone, opened the app, and told me he wanted to watch a specific show on Netflix.

Unprompted. Nobody put a device in front of him. Nobody cued him. He had something he wanted, and he came and got the tool to say it. Sixteen years, and that had never happened. Not once, with any device, in any therapy.

When saying it gets cheap enough, they start saying it on their own.

The point people miss

He still does the thinking. Every single time.

A fair question deserves a straight answer: if the sentences are pre-made, is he really communicating? Yes, and here is why. Every utterance still runs the same cognitive chain it always did: he has to want something, decide to say it, hold it in mind, find it among his boards, and choose it. Intention, formulation, retrieval, selection. That is communication work, and it is all still his.

Why pre-written phrases work

They aren't canned speech. They're his sentences, harvested from his life.

Spoken conversation runs at 125–185 words a minute. A person assembling word-by-word on an AAC board typically manages 12 to 18. Nobody can hold a hallway conversation across a tenfold gap, so turns get skipped, partners move on, and the user learns to say less. But most of a real day is the same hundred sentences, and those can cost one tap instead of forty.

Consider how you speak: you don't spell words letter-by-letter when you talk. You retrieve whole phrases you've said a thousand times, and nobody accuses you of not thinking. EZvoxa gives Evan the same privilege. And every phrase in his app was authored by the people who know him best, from his actual life, then spoken in the voice he chose. When something new needs saying, the sentence builder and the speller are one tap down. Pre-written first does not mean pre-written only.

The cost of being unheard

Imagine mastering something, then being asked to prove it again. Every day. For years.

A nonverbal child lives with two frustrations most of us never taste. The first: being asked to do the same thing over and over, long after they've mastered it, because performing it on demand is the only proof they're allowed to give. The second is worse: feeling something and having no way to share it. Pain. Joy. Fear. An opinion. A joke. It stays inside. All of it. For years.

You know where that pressure goes, because you see it in your buildings every week: acting out. Refusing to participate. For some kids, on some days, violent outbursts. We label the behavior, we chart it, we write plans for it. But so often the behavior isn't the problem. The behavior is the message. It is what "nobody can hear me" looks like from the outside.

What it actually means

It opens lines of communication that did not exist before.

Evan cannot take a phone call. Until this year, he had never once heard his mother speak to him through his own device. Now, when they're apart, she records a voice message on her phone, and it arrives on his and plays for him, unprompted, in her actual voice. Not a notification he has to decode. His mom, out loud, saying she loves him.

And he can answer. He can tap a phrase and his talker says it aloud, or he can send it to her as a text, straight from the app, without needing the Messages app his locked phone can't open.

Reason two · speed

Conversation at the pace of life, not the pace of a menu tree.

Word-by-word assembly runs at 12–18 words a minute against speech at 125–185. In a classroom, that tenfold gap decides everything: by the time the sentence is built, the discussion moved on, the group finished, the moment passed. Faster isn't a convenience feature for these students. Faster is the difference between participating and watching.

One tap per sentence means Evan answers while the question still hangs in the air. He takes his turn. He asks for help before frustration becomes behavior. He does his math and his simile homework in the period they were assigned. Quicker and more efficient isn't the sales pitch. It's the entire mechanism by which everything else in this room's job gets easier.

Reason three · the dedicated device, reinvented

A phone can be a dedicated talker. Evan's is. Here's exactly how.

The credibility of a $4,000 SGD is that it does one thing: it talks, and a student can't wander off into games. A phone can be given the same integrity. A communication device that grows with them and meets them where they are.

That's what "meets them where they are" actually means. A non-reader runs on pictures. A student who needs speed runs on one-tap phrases. For technology and district staff

How the app is built, and what that means at school.

I am not a security company, so instead of assurances, here is exactly how the app is put together and what that does and does not protect. Everything below can be checked by your own staff.

  • ✓
    One file, with no third-party code running inside it.

    The whole app is a single web page. No advertising libraries, no analytics packages, no tracking scripts, no outside code loaded while it runs. Your staff can confirm it in a minute: open the app with the browser's network inspector on and watch it make no outside requests at all. It was tested with all network access switched off and it launched and ran completely, which is only possible because nothing outside is required to run it.

  • ✓
    There is nothing stored about a student to steal.

    No accounts, no usernames, no student passwords, no email addresses, no grades, no attendance, no assessment data, no identifiers of any kind. The app cannot tell you who is using it. Boards, phrases and photographs live in the device's own storage and nowhere else.

  • ✓
    Photographs never leave the device.

    A photo taken for a button is written to that device only. Never uploaded, never included in anything shared between devices, and gone when the person is removed or the app is deleted.

  • ✓
    The device is what is locked, and schools already know how.

    On a dedicated device the app runs under iOS Guided Access, so the student cannot leave the app, browse the internet, or reach anything else on the device. The app adds its own start screen and close step so a device in a pocket cannot press its own buttons. Editing sits behind a password, so a student cannot change or delete their own board.

  • ✓
    What travels, and only when the feature is used.

    The AI voice sends the text of a tapped phrase to be spoken, over an encrypted connection, cached so a repeat is not sent again. An emergency alert sends the message and location at that moment to the family contacts a parent set up. A text message goes to the family number a parent entered. Nothing else is transmitted, and none of it is attached to a name.

  • ✓
    Where the secrets live.

    No phone numbers, keys or credentials exist anywhere inside the app. They are held as server settings the app cannot read, and the app sends an internal reference, never a number. Everything is served over HTTPS.

  • ✓
    How updates arrive.

    The app updates itself from our hosting the next time it is opened. No installer, nothing for your staff to push, and no way for code from anywhere else to load into it.

My recommendation for a pilot

Run the pilot devices with sync off and no emergency contacts configured.

In that state every device is entirely self-contained. It stores nothing off the device, sends nothing anywhere, and needs no network at all. It is the simplest configuration to approve and the easiest to verify. Sync and alerts are family features that belong on a parent's own phone, not a classroom tablet.
