# AAC outcomes for school-age children: what the studies actually say

Research slice: OUTCOMES (success rates, strongest studies, abandonment). Prepared for Frank Schaeffer, EZ Voice LLC.

Date read for every source below: 10 September 2026 (the sandbox clock reported 9 September 2026 UTC; nothing was read on any other day). Every number in this file is copied from the abstract or full text named next to it. Where only the abstract was readable it says "abstract only". Where a paper gives no number, none is written.

Access method: PubMed E-utilities (esearch, esummary, efetch) at eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov; PMC full text via efetch db=pmc (the PMC web pages themselves return a reCAPTCHA wall from here); ies.ed.gov (What Works Clearinghouse) pages directly.

## 1. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

### Ganz, Earles-Vollrath, Heath, Parker, Rispoli and Duran (2012). J Autism Dev Disord 42(1):60-74
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21380612/ (abstract only). Effect-size figures below are as reported in the summary table of Iacono, Trembath and Erickson 2016 (full text, PMC5036660, see below), not from the Ganz paper itself.
- Design: meta-analysis of 24 single-case studies of aided AAC (PECS, speech-generating devices, other picture-based systems) with individuals with ASD. Effect size measure: Improvement Rate Difference (IRD).
- Abstract findings: "aided AAC interventions had large effects on targeted behavioral outcomes"; effects larger for communication than other skill categories; PECS and SGD effects larger than other picture systems, which had "small effects".
- Numbers per Iacono 2016, Table 2 (attributed to Ganz et al 2012 as reference 20): overall IRD 0.99; communication 0.99; social skills 0.90; challenging behavior 0.80; academic skills 0.79; PECS and SGD 0.99; other picture systems 0.61. Iacono's Table 2 also lists a companion Ganz analysis (reference 19, 24 studies, 1980-2008) with IRD by age: preschool 0.86, elementary 0.70, secondary 0.64; and by diagnosis: autism 0.83, autism plus DD 0.70, autism plus multiple comorbidities 0.53.
- Participant count: not given in the abstract; not written.

### Ganz, Rispoli, Mason and Hong (2014). Dev Neurorehabil 17(3):184-192
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24102440/ (abstract only; numbers from Iacono 2016 Table 2, reference 23).
- 35 single-case studies, IRD. Largest effects in general education settings. IRD by setting (Iacono table): home 0.70 (n=11 studies), general education 0.84 (n=5), special education 0.71 (n=17), therapy room 0.66 (n=5). By system and outcome: PECS/communication 0.73 (n=15), SGD/communication 0.71 (n=8), other picture systems/communication 0.58 (n=7).

### Ganz, Mason, Goodwyn, Boles, Heath and Davis (2014). Am J Intellect Dev Disabil 119(6):516-535
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25354122/ (abstract only).
- Meta-analysis (IRD) of PECS, SGDs and other picture-based AAC for individuals with ASD. "AAC has small to moderate effects on speech outcomes." SGDs most effective for ASD without comorbid IDD; PECS most effective for ASD with IDD. SGDs and PECS most effective for preschoolers. "No difference was found between systems for elementary-aged and older individuals."

### Ganz, Morin, Foster, Vannest, Genc Tosun, Gregori and Gerow (2017). Augment Altern Commun 33(4):224-238
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28922953/ (abstract only).
- Meta-analysis of single-case research on high-tech AAC (including mobile devices and apps) for individuals with IDD including ASD. 24 publications, 89 A-B phase contrasts, Tau-U. "Overall low to moderate positive effects on social-communication outcomes."

### Ganz, Pustejovsky, Reichle et al. (2023). Augment Altern Commun 39(1):7-22
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36262108/ (abstract only).
- 114 single-case design studies of AAC with school-aged individuals with ASD and/or intellectual disability. Tau(AB) and log response ratio. "Both indices showed positive effects on average, but also exhibited a high degree of heterogeneity." Moderator analyses "detected few differences" by diagnosis, age, communication mode, productive repertoire, or imitation skills. No numeric effect sizes in the abstract; none written.

### Schlosser and Wendt (2008). Am J Speech Lang Pathol 17(3):212-230
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18663107/ (abstract only).
- Systematic review of effects of AAC on speech production in children with autism/PDD-NOS, studies 1975 to May 2007. 9 single-subject studies (27 participants) and 2 group studies (98 participants). "AAC interventions do not impede speech production. In fact, most studies reported an increase in speech production. However, in-depth analyses revealed that the gains were rather modest." Iacono 2016 Table 2 adds: PND for speech outcomes ranged 0-100, with 4 of 21 effects "fairly or highly effective" and 17 of 21 "ineffective".

### Millar, Light and Schlosser (2006). J Speech Lang Hear Res 49(2):248-264
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16671842/ (abstract only).
- Meta-analysis, literature 1975-2003, 23 studies, 67 individuals. 17 studies lacked experimental control. Best-evidence set: 6 studies, 27 cases, ages 2-60, mostly intellectual disability or autism, interventions were manual signs or non-electronic aided systems. "None of the 27 cases demonstrated decreases in speech production as a result of AAC intervention, 11% showed no change, and the majority (89%) demonstrated gains in speech." Gains "for the most part ... modest".
- Note: no speech-generating devices in the best-evidence set.

### Iacono, Trembath and Erickson (2016). Neuropsychiatr Dis Treat 12:2349-2361
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27703354/ ; full text read via PMC5036660 (efetch).
- Review of 17 systematic reviews of AAC for children with autism up to 21 years, published 2000 to March 2016. "Most provided indicators of research quality for included studies, of which only relatively few provided conclusive results. Communication targets tended to be focused on teaching children to make requests. Still, effect size measures for included studies indicated that AAC was effective to highly effective."
- Quality statement in full text: "evidence for AAC as an autism intervention has been identified as emerging only, rather than established by the National Autism Centre (NAC) in the US", and the NAC's second report did not change that classification. "Promises that AAC can support ongoing language development have yet to be demonstrated convincingly for children with autism."

### Holyfield, Drager, Kremkow and Light (2017). Augment Altern Commun 33(4):201-212
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28884601/ (abstract only).
- Systematic review of AAC intervention research for adolescents and adults with ASD. "Results indicate that AAC intervention benefits adolescents and adults with autism spectrum disorder. However, more research is urgently needed." Future work should target functions other than requesting. No study count, participant count or effect size in the abstract; none written.

### Brignell, Chenausky, Song, Zhu, Suo and Morgan (2018). Cochrane Database Syst Rev 11:CD012324
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30395694/ (abstract read; PMC6516977 text via efetch contained the abstract and summary only).
- Cochrane review of communication interventions for minimally verbal children with ASD under 12 (fewer than 30 functional words or unable to use speech alone). Searched to November 2017. Only 2 RCTs met inclusion (154 children, 32 months to 11 years). One was PECS in UK schools (84 children aged 4-11, teacher-delivered); the other a verbal parent-delivered play intervention (70 children).
- GRADE: "we rated the overall quality of the evidence as very low due to risk of bias, imprecision (small sample sizes and wide confidence intervals) and because there was only one trial identified per type of intervention."
- PECS result: children "were significantly more likely to use verbal initiations and PECS symbols immediately postintervention; however, gains were not maintained 10 months later. There was no evidence that AAC improved frequency of speech, verbal expressive vocabulary or children's social communication or pragmatic language immediately postintervention."
- Overall: "neither of the interventions (PECS or FPI) resulted in maintained improvements in spoken or non-verbal communication in most children."
- Note: Kasari 2014 was not included because it compared two active treatments rather than treatment versus control.

### Other reviews read (all abstract only)
- O'Neill, Light and Pope (2018), J Speech Lang Hear Res 61(7):1743-1765, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29931287/ : meta-analysis of aided AAC input (modeling). 26 single-case designs (88 participants) and 2 group designs (103 participants). "highly effective" across ages and disabilities. Calls for research on long-term language development.
- Biggs, Carter and Gilson (2018), Am J Intellect Dev Disabil 123(5):443-473, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30198767/ : 48 studies, 267 participants birth to 21, aided AAC modeling "generally effective".
- Morin et al. (2018), Augment Altern Commun 34(2):104-117, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29697288/ : 18 experiments in 17 single-case studies; high-tech AAC for social-communication in ASD/ID "can be considered an evidence-based practice" by WWC single-case criteria, "although the review of comparison (i.e., alternating treatment) design studies did not indicate that high-tech AAC is significantly better than low-tech AAC."
- Schlosser and Koul (2015), Augment Altern Commun 31(4):285-309, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26170252/ : scoping review of speech-output technologies in ASD. 48 studies (47 single-case, 1 RCT), 187 individuals.
- Sievers, Trembath and Westerveld (2018), Augment Altern Commun 34(3):219-229, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29706101/ : only 7 articles (6 studies) reported predictors of AAC outcome in ASD. Predictors: pre-intervention cognition, ASD severity, verbal imitation, vocabulary comprehension, object use, joint attention, language use.
- Pope, Light and Laubscher (2025), J Autism Dev Disord 55(9):3078-3099, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38848009/ ; full text via PMC12208088: 29 studies. Table 3: NDBI-only single-case Tau-U 0.74 (large); NDBI plus AAC single-case Tau-U 0.85 (very large; 0.81 for "strong certainty" studies only); comparative studies: Kasari 2014 d = 0.29 to 0.62. Only 3 studies directly compared NDBI with and without AAC.
- Privitera et al. (2026), Med Sci (Basel) 14(3):388, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42506357/ : 18 studies, narrative synthesis; "findings were heterogeneous"; calls for standard definitions of minimally verbal and long-term outcomes.

## 2. Randomized trials

### Kasari, Kaiser, Goods, Nietfeld, Mathy, Landa, Murphy and Almirall (2014). J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry 53(6):635-646
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24839882/ ; full text read via PMC4030683 (efetch). Trial NCT01013545.
- SMART design. 61 minimally verbal children with autism, aged 5-8, "fewer than 20 spontaneous different words used during the 20-minute" natural language sample. Randomized to JASP+EMT with or without an SGD; 6 months treatment, 3-month follow-up. Primary outcome: total spontaneous communicative utterances (TSCU) at week 24.
- Result: TSCU at week 24 was 61.9 utterances (95% CI 52.8 to 71.0) with SGD versus 40.3 (95% CI 32.7 to 48.0) without; difference 21.6 (95% CI 10.7 to 32.4), p < 0.01; effect size 0.62. Using spoken utterances only for the SGD group, effect size 0.51, still p < 0.05. 92.1% of TSCU in the SGD group were spoken. Secondary outcomes at week 24: novel words d = 0.29, comments d = 0.44. "By week 36, treatment effects were maintained for TCOM, but attenuated for TSCU and TDWR."
- Attrition: 10% by week 12, 14% by week 24, 25% by week 36. Planned n was 97; 61 enrolled.
- Adding the SGD late (only for slow responders) did not help: 42.7 versus 39.6 utterances, ES 0.10, p = 0.40.

### Almirall et al. (2016). J Clin Child Adolesc Psychol 45(4):442-456
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26954267/ ; full text via PMC4930379.
- Same 61 children, growth to week 36. Adaptive intervention starting with SGD was superior: TSCU area-under-curve effect size 0.67 versus no-SGD, 0.58 versus late-SGD. Novel words: no significant between-group difference (p = 0.12); estimated 8 additional novel words over 6 months with SGD (95% CI -0.2 to 16.3; ES 0.37).

### Kasari, Shire, Shih, Kaiser, Lord, Levato, Smith and Almirall (2025). J Am Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry 64(6):674-685
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39864797/ ; full text via PMC12587618.
- Community SMART, 194 minimally verbal (under 20 functional words) autistic children aged 5-8, DTT versus JASP-EMT delivered at school. No SGD arm in this trial. Primary result: no difference between starting with DTT or JASP-EMT on spontaneous communicative utterances at 16 weeks (p = .41 in abstract; ES -0.1 in text). Best adaptive sequence: average SCU change 7.68 (95% CI 2.13 to 13.24). Relevance: the largest school-delivered trial in this population, and it did not test a device.

### Romski, Sevcik, Adamson, Cheslock, Smith, Barker and Bakeman (2010). J Speech Lang Hear Res 53(2):350-364
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20360461/ (abstract only).
- 68 toddlers with developmental delay and fewer than 10 spoken words, randomized to augmented input, augmented output, or spoken communication intervention; 62 completed. Augmented groups "used augmented and spoken words for the target vocabulary items, whereas children in the SC intervention produced a very small number of spoken words. Vocabulary size was substantially larger for AC-O and AC-I than for SC groups." Conclusion: augmented communication "does not hinder, and actually aids, speech production". No effect sizes in abstract.

### Later RCTs involving SGDs (abstract only unless stated)
- Gilroy, Leader and McCleery (2018), Autism Res 11(12):1701-1711, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30475454/ : 35 school-age children with ASD randomized to SGD app or PECS cards, 4 months in classrooms. Both improved; "these improvements did not differ significantly between the two approaches."
- Gilroy, McCleery and Leader (2023), J Appl Behav Anal 56(3):593-606, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37092868/ : 29 autistic children with ID; function-based approach beat eclectic classroom practice; "significant differences were not observed between outcomes of high- and low-tech forms".
- Thiemann-Bourque, Feldmiller, Hoffman and Johner (2018), J Speech Lang Hear Res 61(8):2045-2061, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30054629/ : 45 nonverbal/minimally verbal preschoolers with ASD plus 95 peers, iPad SGD, peer-mediated. Treated children showed significant increases in rate of communication and reciprocity; gains generalized and maintained. WWC review of this study (see section 6): meets standards without reservations, "No statistically significant positive findings" on the standardized PLS-5 expressive communication outcome.
- Hampton, Kaiser and Fuller (2020), Autism 24(8):2104-2116, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32627570/ : 68 preverbal preschoolers, multi-component intervention including an SGD; "may be effective in improving joint attention skills immediately following intervention and social communication skills 4 months following intervention." Abstract states "up to 30% of children with autism will remain minimally verbal past age 5."
- Binger, Harrington, Hahs-Vaughn and Kent-Walsh (2026), J Speech Lang Hear Res 69(6):2654-2678, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42223368/ : 40 preschoolers needing AAC with relatively intact receptive language; intervention group had significantly greater growth in weighted MLU in symbols versus control.
- Sterrett, Holbrook, Landa, Kaiser and Kasari (2023), Augment Altern Commun 39(1):23-32, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36267016/ : secondary analysis of 31 children; responsiveness to adult SGD models at entry predicted commenting and number of different words post-intervention; children with more joint attention responded to SGD models at higher rates.

## 3. Device abandonment and non-use

### Phillips and Zhao (1993). Assist Technol 5(1):36-45
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10171664/ (abstract only).
- Survey of 227 adults with various disabilities. "29.3% of all devices were completely abandoned." Mobility aids abandoned most. Abandonment highest in the first year and after 5 years. Four factors: lack of consideration of user opinion in selection, easy device procurement, poor device performance, change in user needs or priorities.
- Caveat: adults, all assistive technology, 1993. Not AAC-specific and not children.

### Johnson, Inglebret, Jones and Ray (2006). Augment Altern Commun 22(2):85-99
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17114167/ (abstract only).
- Six focus groups then a 106-question survey returned by 275 ASHA Special Interest Division 12 (AAC) members. Constructs tied to long-term success: Support, Attitude, System characteristics and Fit. Constructs tied to inappropriate abandonment: Not Maintaining/Adjusting the System, Attitude, Lack of Training, Lack of Support, Poor Fit.
- The abstract gives no abandonment percentage. None written.

### Moorcroft, Scarinci and Meyer (2019). Augment Altern Commun 35(3):193-204
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31307237/ (abstract only).
- Interviews with 16 SLPs in Australia who had each seen at least one AAC system rejected or abandoned by a parent of a young child. Six themes: how parents view their child; parents' views on AAC; support networks; capacity of and demands on parents; services provided by SLPs; features of the AAC system. Novel finding: parent grief and loss as a contributor to abandonment. No percentage.
- Companion review, Moorcroft, Scarinci and Meyer (2019), Disabil Rehabil Assist Technol 14(7):710-731, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30070927/ : 43 articles on barriers to low-tech and unaided AAC; systems "significantly underutilized"; most barriers environmental (attitudes and supports of professionals, family, society). No percentage.

### Majeski, Ryan and Nadeau (2025). Assist Technol 37(1):14-21
- URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36576047/ (abstract only).
- Abstract states: "Although the majority of users benefit from these tools, it is reported that roughly one third eventually abandon them." This is the authors' summary of prior literature; the abstract does not cite the study behind "one third", and the full text is on tandfonline.com (blocked). Treat as unverified secondary claim. Qualitative themes for successful SGD use: user-device match, communication partner support, occupational roles and routines.

### Non-use data in children (not abandonment surveys, but the closest measured figures)
- Cockerill et al. (2014), Child Care Health Dev 40(2):149-157, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23656274/ (abstract only). Population cohort of young people with bilateral cerebral palsy, aged 16-18, UK (births 1989-1992). 224 assessed for speech; 72 (32%) had been provided one or more types of AAC, "but in a significant number (75% of 52 recorded) AAC was not used at home, only in school." Conclusion: "most had been provided with AAC but few used it at home for communication."
- Andzik, Chung and Kranak (2016), Augment Altern Commun 32(4):272-281, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27819139/ (abstract only). 23 elementary students observed for 117 hours across school settings. Average 17 opportunities to respond per hour; "in nearly half of these opportunities, students did not have access to their AAC system." Very few spontaneous initiations.
- Andzik, Schaefer, Nichols and Chung (2018), Dev Neurorehabil 21(1):40-47, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28727477/ (abstract only). Over 4,000 special educators in 50 states reported on 15,643 students. "Over half of students utilizing alternative and augmentative communication (AAC) were reported to have non-proficient communication."
- Coan-Brill et al. (2025), Am J Speech Lang Pathol 34(2):605-616, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39879487/ (abstract only). Canadian survey, N = 60 caregivers and clinicians, children with CP: need for AAC over 75%, access roughly equal to need, "only 38% of children used AAC systems", and users mostly used them "to make choices, rather than engaging in meaningful reciprocal interactions". Small, not representative.
- Swisa, Weintraub and Gefen (2026), Disabil Rehabil Assist Technol 21(6):2807-2817, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41865382/ (abstract only). 27 children and young adults aged 2-22, customized assistive devices (mostly daily living and mobility, not AAC), Israel: "The AT abandonment rate was 37%."
- Perfect, Hoskin, Noyek and Davies (2020), Dev Neurorehabil 23(3):145-159, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30987518/ (abstract only). Systematic review of eye-gaze AT in children, 11 articles: "barriers could lead to rejection of the technology even when children were successful using the system."
- Bailey, Parette, Stoner, Angell and Carroll (2006), Lang Speech Hear Serv Sch 37(1):50-60, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16615749/ (abstract only). 6 family members of 7 youth who use AAC devices in school; abstract states device use "in school and home settings is often inconsistent". Themes only, no percentages.

No study found that measures how many school-issued SGDs are abandoned, by whom, or when. Searches run on PubMed: "speech generating device abandonment rate" (0 results), "augmentative alternative communication abandonment speech generating device" (3 results, listed above), "assistive technology abandonment children rate" (3 results, listed above).

## 4. Adolescents (13 to 21) and use over time

- Holyfield et al. 2017 (section 1): AAC benefits adolescents and adults with ASD; more research "urgently needed"; most studies target requesting. No counts in abstract.
- Ganz et al. 2014 (Am J Intellect Dev Disabil, section 1): "No difference was found between systems for elementary-aged and older individuals." Ganz 2012 IRD by age per Iacono table: preschool 0.86, elementary 0.70, secondary 0.64 (effects smaller with age). Ganz 2023: few differences by age across 114 studies.
- Drager et al. (2019), J Intellect Dev Disabil 44(3):321-336, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31263379/ (abstract only). 9 students aged 8-20 with severe disability; VSDs with just-in-time programming; all increased symbolic communication turns (single-subject multiple probe).
- Wendt, Hsu, Simon, Dienhart and Cain (2019), Behav Modif 43(6):898-932, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31422681/ (abstract only). 3 participants aged 14-23 with severe ASD; iPad SGD (SPEAKall!) within a PECS protocol; requesting increased for all three; speech gains mixed.
- Babb, McNaughton, Light, Caron, Wydner and Jung (2020), Augment Altern Commun 36(1):31-42, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32208862/ (abstract only). 4 adolescents with ASD or Down syndrome; video VSDs in a volunteer activity; all met mastery.
- Chavers, Morris, Schlosser and Koul (2021), Am J Speech Lang Pathol 30(6):2476-2491, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34586915/ (abstract only). 3 children aged 7-13 with severe ASD; SGD instruction; strong effects for multistep requesting, medium for small talk; maintained 3 weeks post.
- West et al. (2025), Disabil Rehabil Assist Technol 20(8):2935-2948, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40498103/ (abstract only). 227 families of adolescents aged 13-18 with ASD and/or Down syndrome who use AAC, online survey; families grouped by high, moderate, low AAC usage; family functioning score significantly higher in the high-usage group. The abstract does not give the share in each usage group.
- Cockerill 2014 (section 3): the one population cohort at ages 16-18; 75% of recorded AAC recipients did not use AAC at home.
- Hamm and Mirenda (2006), Augment Altern Commun 22(2):134-147, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17114171/ (abstract only). 8 Canadian adults with developmental disabilities who used AAC technology in school. Post-school outcomes "generally discouraging"; quality of life correlated highly with quality of communication; "the majority of participants and their supporters were very dissatisfied with the lack of AAC and other services that were available to them as young adults."
- Lund and Light (2006, 2007a, 2007b), Augment Altern Commun 22(4):284-299, 23(1):1-15, 23(4):323-335; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17127616/ , https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17364484/ , https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17852054/ (abstracts only). 7 young men aged 19-23 who had used AAC at least 15 years since preschool. Part I abstract: "there are no data on the long-term outcomes of AAC interventions"; outcomes "diverse, with individual variations across all measures". Part II: 3 of 7 used complete, complex syntax; 4 made many syntactic errors. Part III: barriers were attitudes, cultural differences, technology, service delivery; enablers were community and family support, personal characteristics, quality AAC services.
- Masayko, McGowan and Grampurohit (2024), Am J Speech Lang Pathol 33(3):1254-1265, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38319681/ (abstract only). 10 parents of 9 school-aged children who trialed eye-gaze AAC in preschool: "All children in this research continue to use AAC technology in school and most at home." Descriptive, n = 9.
- "Device fatigue": no peer-reviewed source found using that term or measuring declining SGD use over years in the same children. Searches: "augmentative alternative communication school-aged children device use over time follow-up" (0 results), "augmentative communication secondary school students transition adulthood" (0 results), "speech generating device adolescents autism intervention" (results listed above). Not written as a finding.

## 5. Minimally verbal children with autism: how many remain so, and the definitions

- Tager-Flusberg and Kasari (2013), Autism Res 6(6):468-478, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24124067/ ; full text via PMC3869868. "It is currently estimated that about 30% of children with autism spectrum disorder remain minimally verbal, even after receiving years of interventions and a range of educational opportunities." Full text: older statistics said over half failed to acquire spoken language (National Research Council 2001); "about 25-30% will be nonverbal, or only minimally verbal by the time they enter Kindergarten (Anderson et al, 2007)". The paper says "there is little consistency in the definition of this group" and lists definitions used by trials: fewer than 10 intelligible words (Romski 2010), fewer than 20 different words across three samples (Yoder and Stone 2006), fewer than 5 spoken words (Kasari 2008).
- Rose, Trembath, Keen and Paynter (2016), J Intellect Disabil Res 60(5):464-477, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27120989/ (abstract only). Longitudinal cohort, 246 children with ASD in a community early-intervention program (Australia). Prior estimates "vary from 25% to 35%". At exit, 26.3% used "fewer than five spontaneous and functional words" and 36.4% were not using "two word phrases" on direct assessment; parent report: 29.4% not naming three objects consistently, 43.3% not using noun-verb phrases consistently. "More than half of the children who entered the programme with minimal speech exited the programme with a similar language profile." 1.2% to 4.7% regressed.
- Brignell et al. 2018 (Cochrane, section 1): "about 25% to 30% of children with ASD either fail to develop functional language or are minimally verbal"; review definition: fewer than 30 functional words or unable to use speech alone.
- Hampton et al. 2020 (section 2): "About 50% of children with autism are not talking by age 3 and up to 30% of children with autism will remain minimally verbal past age 5."
- Kasari trials (2014, 2025): fewer than 20 spontaneous different or functional words, ages 5-8.
- Pizzano et al. (2024), Autism Res 17(6):1218-1229, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38803132/ (abstract only). 344 minimally verbal children aged 3-8 from two trials; latent profile analysis found three groups: global delays (n = 206) and two groups with variable strengths (n = 95 and n = 43). Point: "minimally verbal" is not one group.

## 6. Speech-generating devices in schools: federal data and What Works Clearinghouse

- WWC product search for "augmentative" with the disabilities filter: "Search Results 0 Results" (https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Search/Products?searchTerm=augmentative, read 10 September 2026). There is no WWC intervention report or practice guide on AAC or SGDs. The all-intervention-reports page (https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/AllInterventionReports) is rendered by script and its listing could not be parsed from here; the keyword searches returned only single-study reviews.
- WWC search "speech-generating" returned 6 products; the four study pages opened:
  - https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/94955 : Thiemann-Bourque et al. 2018 (45 preschool students, RCT). Reviewed February 2026. Rating: "Meets WWC standards without reservations because it is a randomized controlled trial with low attrition." Findings: "No statistically significant positive findings"; PLS-5 Expressive Communication domain "Uncertain effects".
  - https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/94966 : Gevarter and Horan 2019, GoTalk SGD plus prompting and reinforcement, 6 preschool students, single-case design. Reviewed March 2026 for a practice guide. "Meets WWC standards without reservations."
  - https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/95005 : Lorah, Miller and Griffen 2021, peer manding with an SGD, 3 preschool students, single-case. "Meets WWC standards with reservations"; "At least one finding shows promising evidence of effectiveness."

## 7. Communication outcomes by layout type: grid versus visual scene display

- Drager, Light, Speltz, Fallon and Jeffries (2003), J Speech Lang Hear Res 46(2):298-312, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14700373/ (abstract only). 30 typically developing children aged 2;5 to 2;11, 10 per condition: taxonomic grid, schematic grid, integrated scene. Children "performed poorly in all conditions but were able to locate more vocabulary items in the schematic scene condition than the taxonomic grid or schematic grid conditions." Conclusion: "The current design of AAC dynamic display systems appears to be inappropriate for very young children."
- Drager, Light, Carlson, D'Silva, Larsson, Pitkin and Stopper (2004), J Speech Lang Hear Res 47(5):1133-1148, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15603467/ (abstract only). 30 typically developing 3-year-olds, 10 per condition. "After the 1st learning session, children performed significantly better with AAC technologies in a contextual scene format than in a grid format." Light et al. 2019 (full text) adds: 3-year-olds "were more accurate navigating using thumbnails of VSDs rather than isolated symbols on a separate page."
- Both Drager studies used typically developing children, not children with disabilities, and measured locating vocabulary, not communication outcomes.
- Light, Wilkinson, Thiessen, Beukelman and Fager (2019), Augment Altern Commun 35(1):42-55, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30648896/ ; full text via PMC6436972. State-of-the-science review. For children with developmental disabilities: "research supports the use of personalized photo VSDs that include familiar people engaged in meaningful activities, with navigation bars with thumbnail VSDs, located adjacent to the main VSD." For grids: "research supports the use of spatial cues and clustering based on internal symbol colour to facilitate visual searching and selection. Background colour does not seem to facilitate searching for symbols on smaller displays, and may actually distract children." Full text: "beginning communicators with complex communication needs increase significantly the frequency of their communication turns as well as the number of concepts expressed when introduced to AAC technologies that utilize VSDs (e.g., Drager et al., 2018; Holyfield, Caron, Drager, & Light, 2018)". Infants 9-12 months "looked first and looked longest at the photo VSDs compared to the grids" (Light, Drager and Wilkinson 2012, exploratory).
- Drager et al. 2019 (section 4): 9 students aged 8-20 with severe disability increased symbolic communication turns with VSDs plus just-in-time programming (single-subject).
- Wilkinson, Brittlebank, Barwise, Zimmerman and Light (2024), Augment Altern Commun 40(3):155-167, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38786201/ (abstract only). 10 individuals on the autism spectrum and 9 with Down syndrome; VSD with navigation bar; visual fixation latency significantly correlated with motor selection latency. Eye tracking as proxy, not a communication outcome.
- No randomized or group comparison of grid versus VSD on communication outcomes in children with disabilities was found. The VSD evidence in disabled populations is single-case (n = 3 to 9) plus eye-tracking; the grid-versus-scene comparisons with random assignment are in typically developing 2- and 3-year-olds from 2003 and 2004.

## What the evidence actually supports

Aided AAC, including SGDs, reliably increases requesting and simple communication in single-case studies with children with autism, and it does not suppress speech; that much is replicated across dozens of studies and every review says so. The one strong device trial in school-age minimally verbal children, Kasari 2014, is 61 children, showed a moderate effect (d = 0.62) on spontaneous utterances at 6 months that had attenuated by 9 months, and had 25% attrition by follow-up; it has not been replicated with a device, and the 194-child 2025 community trial did not test a device at all. Head-to-head, high-tech has not beaten low-tech: two small RCTs (35 and 29 children) and the Morin 2018 review found no significant difference between SGDs and picture cards. The Cochrane review rated the entire RCT evidence base for minimally verbal children as "very low" quality, with PECS gains gone at 10 months. About 25% to 30% of autistic children stay minimally verbal into school age, and more than half of those who enter early intervention minimally verbal leave it the same way; the definitions vary from under 5 to under 30 words. The layout evidence favors visual scene displays for beginning communicators, but the only randomized grid-versus-scene comparisons are in typically developing 2- and 3-year-olds from 2003 and 2004; in children with disabilities it is single-case work with 3 to 9 participants and eye tracking. The abandonment number everyone quotes (about a third) traces to a 1993 survey of 227 adults across all assistive technology; the AAC-specific abandonment papers (Johnson 2006, Moorcroft 2019) are surveys and interviews of clinicians with no rate. The closest measured non-use figures are a UK cerebral palsy cohort where 75% of recorded AAC recipients did not use it at home, an observation study where elementary students lacked their device in nearly half of communication opportunities, and a national teacher survey where over half of AAC users were rated non-proficient. Nobody has measured how many school-issued SGDs are still in use one, three or five years later, who stops using them, or why; the only long-term outcome studies are 7 and 8 people, and Lund and Light wrote in 2006 that "there are no data on the long-term outcomes of AAC interventions", a statement I found nothing to overturn. There is no federal dataset on SGD issuance or use in schools, and the What Works Clearinghouse has no intervention report on AAC or SGDs; its only SGD reviews are four preschool studies, and the one RCT among them showed no statistically significant positive finding on the standardized outcome.
