# The research behind how elementary-school AAC systems are designed, and how old it is

Prepared for Frank Schaeffer, EZ Voice LLC (EZvoxa). Read date for every source: 10 September 2026 (the session clock showed 9 September 2026 UTC; all pages were read in the same session).

How to read this document. Every claim below is tied to a source that was actually opened, with its URL. "Abstract only" means the PubMed abstract was read and the full paper was not. "Citation only" means the paper itself could not be opened; its title, authors, journal, year and DOI were copied from the reference list of a peer-reviewed paper that was opened (the PMID of that paper is given). "Not checked" means no primary source could be reached, so the claim is unverified. Nothing here comes from forums, blogs, Wikipedia or resellers.

Network note. PubMed E-utilities worked throughout. The AAC journal (tandfonline.com), ASHA publications (pubs.asha.org), SAGE, Wiley, ERIC, Google Scholar (rate-limited after the first request), CrossRef, Semantic Scholar and Europe PMC were all unreachable. PMC full text worked for three articles and then began returning a reCAPTCHA page. minspeak.com serves a captcha gate. pecsusa.com, cpec.org.au (PODD), aacandautism.com (LAMP) and goboardmaker.com were refused by the proxy.

## 1. Semantic Compaction / Minspeak / Unity (PRC-Saltillo)

**Who and when (maker's own statements).**

- "Developed in the early 1980s by Dr. Bruce Baker, Minspeak uses multi-meaning symbols to represent words." Source: PRC-Saltillo, Minspeak page, https://prc-saltillo.com/vocabularies/minspeak (read 10 September 2026).
- PRC-Saltillo company timeline: "1982: Prentke Romich teams with Bruce Baker of SEMANTIC COMPACTION to incorporate the MINSPEAK vocabulary in PRC devices." "1983: The MINSPEAK 1 (a repackaged Express 3 with MINSPEAK) is officially introduced at the 1st MINSPEAK Conference." "1993: PRC receives U.S. Department of Education grant for Unity development." "1995: As a result of the grant, PRC launches Unity, the foundation for the vocabulary in most PRC products." Source: https://prc-saltillo.com/why-prc-saltillo/history (read 10 September 2026).
- Bruce Baker's original 1982 article (often cited as appearing in Byte magazine): not checked. No primary copy could be reached, and minspeak.com is behind a captcha gate (blocked).

**Design theory (maker's own statement).** The Minspeak page lists the principles as: multi-meaning symbols ("Fewer symbols representing a robust vocabulary"); symbol families (vocabulary organized semantically, so "eat," "hungry," and "food" sit under the same symbol); motor planning ("Words have unique motor sequences, helping users build muscle memory"); word-based vocabulary; core vocabulary; "language for life." Unity is described as "one of the most widely used Minspeak applications," designed with "a consistent symbol layout and predictable symbol sequences" to build "automaticity." Source: https://prc-saltillo.com/vocabularies/minspeak (read 10 September 2026). The page cites no studies.

**Published research found (PubMed).** A PubMed search for the exact term "Minspeak" returns three papers (search run 10 September 2026 at https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/esearch.fcgi?db=pubmed&term=%22Minspeak%22):

- van der Merwe E, Alant E. Associations with Minspeak icons. J Commun Disord. 2004;37(3):255-274. PMID 15063146. Abstract only. 480 able-bodied South African tertiary students each completed a questionnaire on one of the 12 core Unity icons; some icons and their encoded words "might be used successfully" locally, others "need to be adapted." This is a study of adults' associations, not of children learning the system. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15063146/
- Mathisen B, et al. Using MINSPEAK: a case study of a preschool child with complex communication needs. Disabil Rehabil Assist Technol. 2009;4(5):376-383. PMID 19484639. Abstract only. A single-child pilot; the authors call it "this modest case study." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19484639/
- Chompoobutr S, et al. 2013, a Thai picture-based system (PMID 23244464); not about Minspeak users.

A PubMed search for "iconic encoding" (the technical name for Minspeak-style sequences) returns two papers, one of which is directly on children:

- Drager KD, Light JC. A comparison of the performance of 5-year-old children with typical development using iconic encoding in AAC systems with and without icon prediction on a fixed display. Augment Altern Commun. 2010;26(1):12-20. PMID 20196700. Abstract only. Twenty typically developing 5-year-olds, 30 vocabulary items, four sessions. The abstract opens: "Iconic encoding, or the use of a sequence of icons to retrieve a word or phrase from augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) technologies, has previously been shown to be challenging for young children to learn to use." Icon prediction did not improve accuracy. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20196700/
- Light J, Drager K, McCarthy J, et al. Performance of typically developing four- and five-year-old children with AAC systems using different language organization techniques. Augmentative and Alternative Communication. 2004;20:63-88. DOI 10.1080/07434610410001655553. Citation only (copied from the reference lists of PMID 29504809 and PMID 28257530). The paper itself is in the blocked AAC journal.

No independent controlled study of Unity or Minspeak with school-age children was found in PubMed. Thistle and Wilkinson's 2015 survey of 112 SLPs listed "supports for motor planning" among the "areas for future research" (PMID 25892523, abstract only, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25892523/).

**Age of the design basis.** Created 1982 (maker's own timeline). Unity 1995 (maker's own timeline). Most recent primary study located that tests the core mechanism (iconic encoding) with children: Drager and Light 2010, with typically developing 5-year-olds, and it found the approach hard for them.

## 2. Picture Communication Symbols (PCS, Mayer-Johnson, now Tobii Dynavox)

**When created, by whom.** The Tobii Dynavox PCS page says only: "For over 30 years, PCS (Picture Communication Symbols) have empowered people..." and "85,000+ symbols, 25+ languages." It names five libraries (PCS, PCS Country, PCS High Contrast, PCS Sign Language, PCS InContext). It does not name a creator or a year. Source: https://www.tobiidynavox.com/pages/pcs-picture-communication-symbols (read 10 September 2026). The Tobii Dynavox about-us page (https://www.tobiidynavox.com/pages/about-us) contains no PCS history. goboardmaker.com was blocked. The commonly repeated claim that Roxanna Mayer-Johnson created PCS in 1981: not checked; no primary source reached.

What can be shown from the peer-reviewed record is that PCS existed and was being tested by 1989: Mizuko M, Reichle J. Transparency and recall of symbols among intellectually handicapped adults. J Speech Hear Disord. 1989;54(4):627-633. PMID 2478760. Abstract only. Compared Blissymbols, "Picture Communication System (PCS)" and Picsyms with adults with intellectual disability; PCS and Picsyms were "more transparent and easier to learn than Blissymbols." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2478760/

**Research on PCS iconicity and transparency for children (all abstract only).**

- Haupt L, Alant E. The iconicity of picture communication symbols for rural Zulu children. S Afr J Commun Disord. 2002;49:40-49. PMID 14968701. Ten-year-olds, 36 symbols on a commercial overlay without glosses: "2.8% and 11.1% (respectively) of the symbols on the communication overlay emerged as iconic" under strict and lenient scoring. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14968701/
- Basson M, Alant E. The iconicity and ease of learning of picture communication symbols: a study with Afrikaans-speaking children. S Afr J Commun Disord. 2005;52:4-12. PMID 17902397. Typically developing urban 6-year-olds, 16 PCS on an overlay: "the 16 PCS symbols had an iconicity of 12.5% (accuracy score > 50%)"; both trained and untrained groups improved on re-test. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17902397/
- Stephenson J. Iconicity in the development of picture skills: typical development and implications for individuals with severe intellectual disabilities. Augment Altern Commun. 2009;25(3):187-201. PMID 19925311. Review: "although iconicity may have some impact on symbol learning, there are other variables that are likely to be much more important. It is likely that iconicity is not helpful to those learning graphic symbols who have little or no comprehension of spoken language." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19925311/
- Dada S, Huguet A, Bornman J. The iconicity of picture communication symbols for children with English additional language and mild intellectual disability. Augment Altern Commun. 2013;29(4):360-373. PMID 24229339. 30 participants, 16 PCS on a bed-making overlay; "overall, the 16 symbols were relatively iconic to the participants." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24229339/
- Worah S, McNaughton D, Light J, Benedek-Wood E. A comparison of two approaches for representing AAC vocabulary for young children. Int J Speech Lang Pathol. 2015;17(5):460-469. PMID 25592055. Forty typically developing children aged 2.5 to 3.5 randomly assigned to "Developmentally Appropriate Symbols" built from children's own conceptualizations, or PCS; "children in the DAS condition correctly identified more symbols than children trained with the PCS symbols." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25592055/
- Schlosser RW, Koul R, Shane H, Sorce J. Effects of animation on naming and identification across two graphic symbol sets representing verbs and prepositions. J Speech Lang Hear Res. 2014. PMID 24811580. Title only (esummary), not read.
- Tsai MJ, et al. Transparency of and preferences for Picture Communication Symbols and VoiceSymbol among young Taiwanese children with typical development. Augment Altern Commun. 2026 (online ahead of print). PMID 42083339. Thirty Mandarin-speaking children; "no statistically significant difference in transparency" between the two sets; children strongly preferred VoiceSymbol. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42083339/

**Age of the design basis.** In the literature by 1989 (Mizuko and Reichle). First child iconicity studies 2002 to 2005 (South Africa). Most recent primary study 2026 (Tsai, Taiwan, 30 children). No US study was found that tests how well elementary-age children read PCS on a real device screen.

## 3. Core vocabulary approach

**Foundational studies.**

- Beukelman DR, Yorkston KM, Poblete M, Naranjo C. Frequency of word occurrence in communication samples produced by adult communication aid users. J Speech Hear Disord. 1984;49(4):360-367. PMID 6239063. Abstract only. Five nonspeaking adults using Canon Communicators, 14 consecutive days; "The 500 core vocabulary words represented 80% of the total words in the combined communication samples for the 5 subjects." Only 33% of messages could be produced entirely from the core list. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6239063/
- Beukelman, Jones and Rowan 1989 (frequency of word usage by nondisabled preschool peers): not checked. Not in PubMed, not found in any reachable reference list.
- Marvin CA, Beukelman DR, Bilyeu D. Vocabulary-use patterns in preschool children: Effects of context and time sampling. Augmentative and Alternative Communication. 1994;10(4):224-236. DOI 10.1080/07434619412331276930. Citation only (reference list of PMID 37988656). Sample size and measures: not checked.
- Banajee M, Dicarlo C, Stricklin SB. Core vocabulary determination for toddlers. Augmentative and Alternative Communication. 2003;19(2):67-73. DOI 10.1080/0743461031000112034. Citation only (reference list of PMID 37988656). Sample size and measures: not checked; the journal is blocked.
- Trembath D, Balandin S, Togher L. Vocabulary selection for Australian children who use augmentative and alternative communication. J Intellect Dev Disabil. 2007;32(4):291-301. PMID 18049973. Abstract only. Six typically developing preschoolers; "a small core vocabulary comprising frequently and commonly used words, together with large and highly individualised fringe vocabularies." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18049973/

**Recent primary work that questions the approach for young children (abstract only).**

- Laubscher E, Light J. Core vocabulary lists for young children and considerations for early language development: a narrative review. Augment Altern Commun. 2020;36(1):43-53. PMID 32172598. "core word lists may under-emphasize many of the types of words that predominate in early expressive vocabulary; these lists may not be the most appropriate resources to guide AAC system design and instruction for early symbolic communicators." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32172598/
- Frick Semmler BJ, Bean A, Wagner L. Examining core vocabulary with language development for early symbolic communicators. Int J Speech Lang Pathol. 2024;26(1):28-37. PMID 36650985. Seven core lists examined; on average the words on them appear in typical development at 25, 27, 37, 45, 47, 50 and 66 months; "Focussing on core words when working with early symbolic communicators who require AAC may lead to limited and variable lexicons with wide gaps." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36650985/
- Binger C, et al. How Toddlers Use Core and Fringe Vocabulary: What's in an Utterance? Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2024;33(4):1718-1747. PMID 38507571. 112 transcripts of 2.5-year-olds; "relying on word frequency counts is an insufficient basis for selecting vocabulary for aided preliterate communicators." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38507571/

**Products that state they are built on it (maker pages, read 10 September 2026).**

- WordPower (Inman Innovations, sold by PRC-Saltillo): "Built around core vocabulary (those super-useful words we all use every day)... from 25 buttons to 144." The page credits Nancy Inman and "its roots at Kennedy Krieger Institute." No study is cited. https://prc-saltillo.com/vocabularies/wordpower
- Unity / Minspeak: "Core vocabulary: Focuses on the most commonly used words in everyday communication." No study cited. https://prc-saltillo.com/vocabularies/minspeak
- LAMP Words for Life: "4,500 frequently used core and fringe words." No study cited. https://prc-saltillo.com/apps/lamp-wfl
- Core First (Tobii Dynavox): the TD Snap page lists "Core First, Motor Plan, Express, Text, Scanning and Aphasia" as page sets and lists Core First languages. No research basis is stated on the page. https://www.tobiidynavox.com/products/td-snap
- Crescendo (AssistiveWare): the Proloquo2Go page says "Proloquo2Go's research-based Crescendo vocabulary, grammar support, and keyboard access promote language growth." No study is cited on that page. https://www.assistiveware.com/products/proloquo2go

**Age of the design basis.** Adult data 1984; preschool data 1994 and 2003 (both citation only); the 2003 toddler list is the one most products point to. Most recent primary studies (2020, 2024, 2024) argue that frequency lists are the wrong starting point for young beginning communicators.

## 4. LAMP (Language Acquisition through Motor Planning)

**Who and when (maker's own statements).**

- PRC-Saltillo timeline: "2007: PRC launches Language Acquisition through Motor Planning (LAMP) therapeutic approach workshops." "2009: The Center for AAC & Autism is established to promote Language Acquisition through Motor Planning (LAMP)." "2012: PRC releases its first app, LAMP Words for Life." Source: https://prc-saltillo.com/why-prc-saltillo/history (read 10 September 2026).
- The PRC Minspeak page quotes "John Halloran, M.S., CCC-SLP" on the method. Source: https://prc-saltillo.com/vocabularies/minspeak. No authorship of a founding paper is stated on any PRC page that was read.

**What the makers say the basis is.** The LAMP Words for Life site: "LAMP is a therapeutic approach based on neurological and motor learning principles that uses a speech generating device to provide opportunities to learn language in engaging activities by using a consistent motor plan to say words and getting a natural response." The "What is LAMP?" page lists five principles: Readiness to Learn, Joint Engagement, Consistent and Unique Motor Patterns, Auditory Signals, Natural Consequences. It says "Research indicates that the amount of joint engagement is directly related to language and communication development," with no citation. Sources: https://lampwflapp.com/ and https://lampwflapp.com/about (read 10 September 2026). The PRC app page calls it "the science-backed Language Acquisition through Motor Planning (LAMP) approach," with no citation: https://prc-saltillo.com/apps/lamp-wfl. The Center for AAC & Autism site (aacandautism.com), where any evidence page would sit, was blocked.

**Independent studies.** PubMed searches on 10 September 2026 returned nothing for a LAMP AAC study: "LAMP" in title with autism or augmentative (0), "Words for Life" in title (0), "Halloran J" with autism, augmentative or speech-language (0), "motor planning" with augmentative and autism or children (3 hits, none about LAMP). The Bedwani, Bruck and Costley 2015 study sometimes cited for LAMP: not checked; not in PubMed and its journal was unreachable.

The nearest peer-reviewed evidence is on the motor-learning principle itself, not on LAMP as a package (all abstract only):

- Thistle JJ, Holmes SA, Horn MM, Reum AM. Consistent Symbol Location Affects Motor Learning in Preschoolers Without Disabilities. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2018;27(3):1010-1017. PMID 29860450. 24 preschoolers without disabilities (12 per condition), five sessions; consistent-location group faster by session five; "replication with children who use AAC is critical." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29860450/
- Dukhovny E, Gahl S. Manual motor-plan similarity affects lexical recall on a speech-generating device. J Commun Disord. 2014;48:52-60. PMID 24630143. Typical adult talkers. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24630143/
- Dukhovny E, Zhou Y. Effects of icon size and location on speed and accuracy of SGD access. Augment Altern Commun. 2016;32(4):241-248. PMID 27762147. Adults without disabilities; location-centred training (six small icons on a 40-location screen) beat size-centred training (six large icons). https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27762147/
- Dukhovny E, Thistle JJ. An exploration of motor learning concepts relevant to use of speech-generating devices. Assist Technol. 2019;31(3):126-132. PMID 29131709. Review: "There is some evidence that application of motor memory principles may improve effectiveness." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29131709/
- Brock KL, Jenkins H, Dukhovny E. The effects of symbol format and symbol placement on identification accuracy and activation time. Augment Altern Commun. 2026;42(1):14-31. PMID 40574552. 42 adults without disabilities. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40574552/

**Age of the design basis.** Workshops 2007, centre 2009, app 2012 (maker timeline). No peer-reviewed primary study of LAMP itself was reachable. The motor-consistency principle has one small study with preschoolers without disabilities (2018) and several adult studies (2014 to 2026).

## 6. PECS (Picture Exchange Communication System, Bondy and Frost)

**Origin.** The earliest PECS paper in PubMed is Bondy AS, Frost LA. Mands across the water: A report on the application of the picture-exchange communication system in Peru. Behav Anal. 1993;16(1):123-128. PMID 22478140 (PMC2733579; record only). The 1994 Focus on Autistic Behavior paper usually given as the origin: not checked. Full descriptions by the developers: Bondy AS, Frost LA. Semin Speech Lang. 1998;19(4):373-388. PMID 9857393, abstract only ("Its theoretical roots combine principles from applied behavior analysis and guidelines established within the field of alternative and augmentative communication"; affiliation Pyramid Educational Consultants). Bondy A, Frost L. Behav Modif. 2001;25(5):725-744. PMID 11573337, abstract only. pecsusa.com blocked.

**Main systematic reviews and meta-analyses (abstract only).**

- Flippin M, Reszka S, Watson LR. Effectiveness of the Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS) on communication and speech for children with autism spectrum disorders: a meta-analysis. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2010;19(2):178-195. PMID 20181849. Literature 1994 to June 2009; "Eight single-subject experiments (18 participants) and 3 group studies (95 PECS participants, 65 in other intervention/control)"; "PECS is a promising but not yet established evidence-based intervention... ages 1-11 years. Small to moderate gains in communication... Gains in speech were small to negative." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20181849/
- Ganz JB, Davis JL, Lund EM, Goodwyn FD, Simpson RL. Meta-analysis of PECS with individuals with ASD. Res Dev Disabil. 2012;33(2):406-418. PMID 22119688. Improvement Rate Difference method; "PECS is a promising intervention method"; "preschool children and those with autism generally showed the strongest training effects." Numeric effect sizes are not in the abstract. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22119688/
- Ganz JB, Earles-Vollrath TL, Heath AK, Parker RI, Rispoli MJ, Duran JB. A meta-analysis of single case research studies on aided AAC systems with individuals with ASD. J Autism Dev Disord. 2012;42(1):60-74. PMID 21380612. 24 single-case studies; "aided AAC interventions had large effects"; "Effects of the Picture Exchange Communication System and speech-generating devices were larger than those for other picture-based systems." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21380612/
- Forbes HJ, Travers JC, Vickers Johnson J. A systematic review of acquisition and mastery of skills taught using the Picture Exchange Communication System. Augment Altern Commun. 2024;40(2):100-114. PMID 38300727. "most participants mastered only the first two phases of PECS"; in Phase III, "participants... learned to select from an average array of three symbols, which may limit the range of messages they can communicate." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38300727/
- Huang H, et al. Efficacy of the Picture Exchange Communication System for children with autism in Mainland China: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Res Dev Disabil. 2026;168:105190. PMID 41442835. 37 RCTs (34 pooled, n = 2343); overall SMD 0.95 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.13); communication SMD 0.94 (0.67 to 1.21); language development not significant, SMD 0.44 (−0.53 to 1.41). Chinese clinical settings; the authors note educational-setting studies were few. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41442835/

**Government review.** The IES What Works Clearinghouse search for "Picture Exchange Communication System" returns one item, a 2024 review of a single study of three US preschoolers on vocalisations in Phase IV, and no intervention report. https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Search/Products?searchTerm=Picture%20Exchange%20Communication%20System (read 10 September 2026). A WWC search for "augmentative and alternative communication" returned 0 results.

**Age of the design basis.** 1993 to 1994. Strongest US-relevant syntheses 2010 to 2012; a 2024 review shows most learners stop at the first two phases; the 2026 meta-analysis is large but drawn entirely from Chinese clinical trials.

## 7. Aided language stimulation / aided AAC modelling

**What it is.** Modelling on the child's own AAC system while speaking. O'Neill, Light and Pope define the family of terms as "aided AAC modeling, aided language modeling, aided language stimulation, augmented input" (PMID 29931287, abstract).

**Foundational paper.** Goossens' C. Aided communication intervention before assessment: A case study of a child with cerebral palsy. Augmentative and Alternative Communication. 1989;5(1):14-26. DOI 10.1080/07434618912331274926. Citation only (reference lists of PMID 34307883, 33789063 and 31868037). A single case study; the journal is blocked.

**Primary studies (abstract only).**

- Drager KD, et al. The effect of aided language modeling on symbol comprehension and production in 2 preschoolers with autism. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2006;15(2):112-125. PMID 16782684. Two children; multiple baseline. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16782684/
- Binger C, Light J. The effect of aided AAC modeling on the expression of multi-symbol messages by preschoolers who use AAC. Augment Altern Commun. 2007;23(1):30-43. PMID 17364486. Five preschoolers; four learned to produce multi-symbol messages. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17364486/
- Dada S, Alant E. The effect of aided language stimulation on vocabulary acquisition in children with little or no functional speech. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2009;18(1):50-64. PMID 19106207. Four children, three weeks; all acquired the 24 target items. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19106207/
- Laher Z, Dada S. Aided language stimulation... a comparison of two dosages. Augment Altern Commun. 2023;39(2):96-109. PMID 36939217. Six participants; 70% dosage worked for all six, 40% for two. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36939217/

**Reviews.**

- Sennott SC, Light JC, McNaughton D. AAC modeling intervention research review. Research and Practice for Persons with Severe Disabilities. 2016;41(2):101-115. DOI 10.1177/1540796916638822. Citation only (reference lists of PMID 33789063 and PMID 31868037). SAGE is blocked; its findings could not be read.
- O'Neill T, Light J, Pope L. Effects of Interventions That Include Aided AAC Input on the Communication of Individuals With Complex Communication Needs: A Meta-Analysis. J Speech Lang Hear Res. 2018;61(7):1743-1765. PMID 29931287. Abstract only. "26 single-case experimental designs (88 participants) and 2 group experimental designs (103 participants)"; "highly effective across participants of various ages"; the interventions "typically included aided AAC input in conjunction with expectant delay, direct prompting... contingent responding, and open-ended questions." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29931287/
- Chazin KT, Ledford JR, Pak NS. A Systematic Review of Augmented Input Interventions and Exploratory Analysis of Moderators. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2021;30(3):1210-1223. PMID 33789063. Abstract only. 99 single-case designs in 26 sources; "two thirds of designs indicated that augmented input was effective"; "augmented input was inconsistently effective as a stand-alone intervention"; "high risk of publication bias, with peer-reviewed sources twice as likely to include positive effects than dissertations and theses." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33789063/

**Age of the design basis.** 1989 (single case). Body of evidence 2006 onward, nearly all single-case designs with two to six children. Most recent syntheses 2018 and 2021; the 2021 review is more cautious than the 2018 one.

## 8. Visual scene displays (VSDs) versus grid displays (Penn State, Light, Drager, McNaughton)

This is the line of work that argues grids of small symbol cells are hard for young children.

**Foundational studies (abstract only unless noted).**

- Light JC, Drager KD. Improving the design of augmentative and alternative technologies for young children. Assist Technol. 2002;14(1):17-32. PMID 12739847. Sets out seven design requirements, including "be easy for children to learn and effortless for children to use" and "grow easily and seamlessly with children as they develop." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12739847/
- Drager KD, Light JC, Speltz JC, Fallon KA, Jeffries LZ. The performance of typically developing 2 1/2-year-olds on dynamic display AAC technologies with different system layouts and language organizations. J Speech Lang Hear Res. 2003;46(2):298-312. PMID 14700373. Thirty typically developing children aged 2;5 to 2;11, ten per condition (taxonomic grid, schematic grid, integrated scene), 12 vocabulary items, four learning sessions plus one generalization session. "The current generation of AAC technologies is largely based on conceptual models of adults who are not disabled (J. Light & P. Lindsay, 1991)." "the children performed poorly in all conditions but were able to locate more vocabulary items in the schematic scene condition than the taxonomic grid or schematic grid conditions." "The current design of AAC dynamic display systems appears to be inappropriate for very young children." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14700373/
- Drager KD, Light JC, Carlson R, et al. Learning of dynamic display AAC technologies by typically developing 3-year-olds: effect of different layouts and menu approaches. J Speech Lang Hear Res. 2004;47(5):1133-1148. PMID 15603467. Ten children per condition, three conditions. "children had difficulty with all of the technologies on initial exposure. After the 1st learning session, children performed significantly better with AAC technologies in a contextual scene format than in a grid format." https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15603467/

## 9. Symbols per screen, symbol size and navigation depth for children

No reachable primary study sets a number of symbols per screen or a minimum symbol size for elementary-age children. What exists (all abstract only):

- Layout and organisation: Drager 2003 and 2004 (see Q8) tested grid versus scene with 12 items; they did not vary cell count or cell size.
- Array size used in the colour and arrangement studies was 12 or 16 cells: Wilkinson K, Carlin M, Thistle J. The role of color cues in facilitating accurate and rapid location of aided symbols by children with and without Down syndrome. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2008;17(2):179-193. PMID 18448605. - Thistle JJ, Wilkinson K. The effects of color cues on typically developing preschoolers' speed of locating a target line drawing. Am J Speech Lang Pathol. 2009;18(3):231-240. PMID 19332524. Fifteen children aged 4;2 to 5;4 and fifteen aged 2;10 to 3;11; array of 12; foreground colour helped; background-only colour slowed the younger children. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19332524/

## What this means

The systems handed to elementary students in the US rest on design ideas that are twenty to forty years old. Minspeak dates to 1982, Unity to 1995, PCS to the 1980s, PECS to 1993, and the core-vocabulary premise to a 1984 adult study and preschool lists from 1994 and 2003. The maker pages for Unity, LAMP, WordPower, Core First and Crescendo describe their design principles but cite no studies; the only peer-reviewed tests of Minspeak-style encoding with children (2004 and 2010, typically developing 4- and 5-year-olds) found it hard for them. LAMP and PODD have no efficacy study indexed in PubMed at all; the nearest evidence for LAMP is one 2018 study of 24 preschoolers without disabilities showing that keeping a symbol in the same place makes it faster to find by the fifth session. PECS has the deepest evidence base, but the US-relevant meta-analysis (2010) rested on 18 single-case and 95 group participants and called it "promising but not yet established," and a 2024 review found most learners never get past the first two of its six phases. The argument that grids of small cells are wrong for young children comes from two Penn State studies in 2003 and 2004 with 30 typically developing toddlers each, ten per condition; the same group has kept producing VSD studies through 2026, but they are single-case designs with three to nine children, and three other labs (2017, 2020, 2025) found grids and scenes about equal once a child had some AAC experience. The 2019 state-of-the-science review from that group gives only two grid rules with research support for children: spatial cues, and clustering symbols by their internal colour; it says background colour may distract. No reachable study has ever set a symbol count per screen, a symbol size, or a navigation depth for children; the child studies that exist used 12- to 16-cell arrays and typically developing preschoolers. The core-word premise that underlies most current products has been challenged by its own research community since 2020 on the ground that frequency lists leave out the words toddlers actually say first. Aided modelling by the adult partner is the practice with the most consistent support, but even there the 2021 review found it unreliable on its own and flagged publication bias. In short, the design basis is old and thin, the makers do not cite it on their product pages, and the parts of it that were tested with children were mostly tested with children who did not need AAC.
