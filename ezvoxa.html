Evan's story

He didn't need a better device. He needed a different one.

Evan and me, this summer.

My son Evan was born in August 2010. I am not a speech pathologist. I am a father who has spent the past 16 years in silence with my son, learning what every look, every nuance of his face, his eyes and his body language are trying to tell me.

We did not know for certain what we were dealing with until Evan was about 7. That is when the diagnosis of Speech Apraxia came into focus. His mind knows what he wants to say, but the signal never gets from that part of his brain to his vocal cords to produce the words.

The device he was given worked. A speech therapist introduced him to a real AAC device, an iPad running serious and respected software, billed at over four thousand dollars. It taught a small boy that words exist, that they can be chosen, that they can be put together to mean something. I'm grateful for it. It gave him language. But it was built for the boy he was. And he grew up.

Given the choice, Evan ignored the device and went back to grunting and pointing, the currency he and I had traded his whole life. It was faster. And when I made him use it, I watched the frustration build in his face as he navigated in and out of a dozen screens to construct something as simple as: "I'm tired, can I go to bed." A sentence any other teenager says in two seconds cost him a minute of menu-diving. Every night. For a thought he'd had a thousand times.

He wasn't failing the device. The device was pricing him out of his own voice. And he responded the way anyone would: he stopped paying.

I didn't design this app. Evan guided me to it. When something in the app wasted his time, he told me the only way he can: he stopped using it. Every one of those silences was a design review, and I treated it like one. This is not a cute thing a father made for his son. It is hundreds of design decisions, each one traced to a way to help unlock the mind of a person who cannot speak.

When it came time to choose how he would sound, I didn't pick. I played him voices one after another and watched his face. He reached out and pointed at one. It wasn't close. For the first time, the voice coming out of his device was one he chose. A teenage boy's voice, not a grown man's, not a robot's.

Then one day he came to me, asked for my phone, opened the app, and told me he wanted to watch a specific show on Netflix. Unprompted. Nobody put a device in front of him. Nobody cued him. He had something he wanted, and he came and got the tool to say it. Sixteen years, and that had never happened. Not once, with any device, in any therapy.

He uses it every single day. He turned sixteen with it in his pocket.

Frank Schaeffer, Fort Pierce, Florida
