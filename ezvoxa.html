Evan Zachary’s story

He didn’t need a better device. He needed one that would grow with him.

This is not a story about an app first. It is the story of a nonverbal teenager who taught his father to stop asking him to meet the world where it was — and start meeting him where he already was.

01 · Who is Evan?

Meet the kid behind all of this.

Evan is my third son and his mother Raniyah’s first. He is nonverbal. He has been mainstreamed with his peers since first grade. Socially, he has always found a way in — eye contact, expression, body language, presence.

Academically, those same tools are not enough. You cannot explain an answer with eye contact. You cannot tell a teacher which part you understand with body language. For a while, frustration did not make Evan act out. It looked more like he simply shut down.

And yet, if you watched him closely, there were signs that something much bigger was still happening inside him.

The ocean has always been one of the places where Evan seems most at peace. “He could stand there for hours. It’s like he’s listening to God.”

A life unfolding.

Not a disability timeline. A son, brother, student, beach kid and teenager — growing up in a family that kept learning him.

EZ
Before we knew the story

His name came first.

Raniyah chose Evan. I asked for the middle name, and Zachary came to me. I knew the biblical story of Zechariah. I was not looking for a parallel to a life we had not lived yet.

Evan is associated with the idea that God is gracious. Zachary comes from Zechariah: the Lord remembers.

In Luke, Zechariah loses his spoken voice. When it matters, he asks for a writing tablet and makes himself understood another way.

I knew that story. What I did not know was that my own son would be nonverbal — or that one day he would need a “tablet” of his own.

God knew. I just heard Him.

02 · How EZvoxa was born

I thought I was going to be his teacher.

After feeling that Evan’s development — especially his communication — had stalled for a couple of years, I homeschooled him for 10th grade. I wanted to get him initiating again instead of constantly waiting to be prompted.

What I started noticing

He kept finding his way back to places on the iPad I could not find myself.

Settings. Accessibility. Diagnostic screens. Coding-related areas. I would close dozens of windows at night, and the next day he would navigate until he was right back where he had been.

The clue was already in his hands

He kept teaching himself something.

His iPad would have dozens of windows open — not games, but Settings, Accessibility menus, diagnostic screens, even coding-related areas I had never seen before.

At night I would close everything to save the battery. The next day he would work his way right back to the same obscure place.

I stopped trying to decide exactly what he was learning. The important thing was that he wanted to learn.

The breakthrough came when I stopped asking Evan to prove what he knew the way I had been taught to prove knowledge.

What if the problem was not the way he learned?

What if the problem was the way we expected him to show us that he had learned?

How much potential do we miss because we do not recognize the evidence?

A diagnosis, a test, a Google search, a worksheet — all can tell us something. None of them can tell us everything that is inside a child.

What makes our way right and theirs wrong?

Professionals bring expertise in their disciplines. Parents bring an expertise nobody else can: years of watching the child when nobody is testing, prompting, scoring, or evaluating them.

We need to be the students. They, our teachers.

Watch what they choose. What they return to. What frustrates them. What makes them light up. Then build from there.

Communication should grow with them

AAC was important. But the goal changed.

Traditional AAC helped teach Evan that words exist and how sentences are built. For a younger child, that learning can be essential. But after years of use, building every thought one word at a time became slow and cumbersome.

The old rhythm

Find a word. Choose it. The device returns home. Find the next category. Find the next word. Return home again. Repeat until the sentence is finished.

HomePeopleIHomeActionswantHomePlacespool

At some point I had to ask myself: Do I need my son to prove he can build a sentence word by word every time — or do I want my son to communicate with me more?

The new rhythm

Start with the thing that matters in real life. Let the visual cue get him close. Then give him natural phrases he can choose quickly.

03 · What EZvoxa is

The pictures are the buttons.

EZvoxa is a phrase-first visual talker built for nonverbal and semi-verbal teens and young adults who can tap a phone. It is designed to make everyday communication faster without taking away the traditional tools they already know.

This is the actual template app

Not Evan’s private board. Not a designer’s mockup.

The public template is the version families will customize. Huge visual tiles. Quick phrases. Hurt. School. Family. Food. Feelings. Morning and night. Places. Sentence Build. The speller. The phone’s own voice. Caregiver editing. Backup & Restore.

What it is not: a medical device. A replacement for 911. A $4,000 speech-generating device. A language curriculum. Or another system your child has to learn to fit into.

Customization is not decoration

The app should conform to the child. Not the other way around.

Use a real photo if that is the clearest cue. Use an avatar if that works better. Use an emoji. Add the actual people they know. Add the places they go. Add the phrases your family really says.

One child may need eight choices. Another may need forty. Evan’s exact board is not the universal answer.

Evan’s process is the roadmap.

I wrote the code. Evan designed the communication by showing me what he would use, what he would ignore and what he returned to on his own.

He went back to school

A door opened.

He began using “I need help” appropriately.

His teacher reported that it became one of the phrases he used most consistently at the right times.

He voluntarily showed the app to his class.

He came to the front of the room and demonstrated his phone and some of its buttons.

His teachers started talking about independence.

One teacher showed him the first part of an assignment that previously might have required hand-over-hand help through each step. He completed the remaining sixteen on his own.

His English assessment rose by nearly 100 points.

At his IEP meeting, multiple teachers were excited about the academic progress and engagement they were seeing.

It feels like Evan is re-emerging.

We cannot claim an app caused an academic score or suddenly put knowledge into him. My belief is different: maybe he finally feels he has a better way to get more of what was already inside him out into the world — and that has made him willing to engage again.

For the parent at the beginning

Your child’s future is not a Google search.

Somewhere a parent is hearing autism, developmental delay or nonverbal for the first time and wondering what happened to the future they imagined.

I cannot tell you what your child’s future will be. Neither can Google. Neither can one evaluation.

Spend the days piecing together the puzzle that helps you discover what God put inside your child.

Watch what they choose. Watch what they return to. Question the assumptions — including your own. Try things that do not fit inside the old lines. Tell the professionals what you see at home. Ask them to help you build from it.

09 · This is the beginning

“You and me, kid. One day we’re gonna change the world.”

I used to tell him that when I tucked him into bed as a little boy. I did not know how. I did not know when. I just believed it.

Maybe EZvoxa helps my son use a voice he did not feel like he had before. But that is not the end.

It is the beginning. The beginning of discovering everything he understands, everything he wants to learn, everything he wants to tell us and every gift God put inside him to share with the world.

The voice was always his. Evan just taught me to build a better way for it to get out.
